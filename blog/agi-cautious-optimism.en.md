# Is AGI Really as Frightening as We Imagine?

In the summer of 2015, a dinner at the Rosewood Sand Hill hotel in Menlo Park helped set OpenAI in motion.

Google had already acquired DeepMind. The people around the table worried that if artificial general intelligence were controlled by a single company, too much capability and influence would be concentrated in one place. OpenAI formally launched later that year as a nonprofit research organization, intended to become a counterweight to Google and to help advanced AI benefit humanity more broadly.

History soon repeated itself in an almost recursive way. In 2021, several former OpenAI employees founded Anthropic after disagreements over the company's direction, its pace of commercialization, and the place of safety research. Anthropic became a public benefit corporation and made reliable, interpretable, and steerable AI central to its mission.

One organization was created to challenge what its founders saw as a dangerous concentration of power. It was later treated by another departing group as the new source of danger. This does not necessarily mean that one side betrayed the other. It reveals a persistent conflict in the AI industry: safety requires patience, while competition punishes it.

## No One Wants to Brake First

OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, and xAI continue to release more capable models. Across the Pacific, teams behind DeepSeek, GLM, Kimi, and other systems are moving just as quickly. The AI race is no longer only a competition for products and users. It is increasingly framed as a contest over national capability, compute supply, and industrial security.

This creates a classic prisoner's dilemma.

Even if one lab sincerely believes that development should slow down, it cannot know that its competitors will do the same. A unilateral pause may mean losing researchers, capital, market share, and technical advantage. Every company can care about safety while continuing to increase model capability. No one wants to bear the cost of braking first, so everyone keeps a foot on the accelerator.

That does not mean every safety commitment is insincere. Good intentions simply do not remove competitive pressure. An organization can genuinely care about safety and genuinely fear falling behind at the same time.

## The Worst-Case Warnings Deserve Attention

A growing number of AI researchers have raised public concerns.

In 2024, Daniel Kokotajlo resigned from OpenAI after losing confidence that the company would act responsibly around the arrival of AGI. He refused to sign departure documents containing a nondisparagement clause because he wanted to preserve his ability to criticize the company. At the time, that decision appeared to put an estimated $1.7 million to $2 million in vested equity at risk.

Stuart Russell, a professor at the University of California, Berkeley and director of the Center for Human-Compatible AI, has also warned for years about losing control of advanced AI. In an interview on The Diary of a CEO, he discussed economic scenarios involving unemployment as high as 80 percent and the possibility that society is not prepared for machines to perform most economically valuable work.

These warnings should not be dismissed as alarmism. Model capabilities are increasing. AI systems can already generate code, operate tools, execute multistep tasks, and run for longer periods with broader permissions. When capability grows faster than control mechanisms, risk grows with it.

Taking those risks seriously, however, does not require treating the worst outcome as a predetermined future.

## My Position: Cautious, but Not Pessimistic

I believe loss-of-control risks deserve serious attention, and I agree that stronger safety mechanisms must develop alongside model capabilities. I do not believe, however, that extreme unemployment or human extinction is already a settled conclusion.

We still do not know when AGI will arrive, what capabilities it will have, whether it can maintain coherent long-term objectives, or how governments and economies will respond. Dismissing every risk would be irresponsible. Presenting the worst scenario as destiny is also unsupported by the available evidence.

Rather than choosing between blind optimism and total pessimism, I prefer cautious optimism: continue developing the technology while demanding clearer boundaries, stronger verification, and more controllable deployment.

## AI Does Not Share a Single Will

Popular culture often presents AI as a unified intelligence that suddenly awakens, shares one objective across every machine, and turns against humanity. Today's AI systems do not form that kind of collective.

Models trained by different companies do not share a common memory. Even the same model can behave differently under different prompts, permissions, tools, and context windows. A failure in one agent does not automatically recruit every other agent into the same behavior.

This does not prove that AI is harmless. Safety researchers do not necessarily expect a coordinated rebellion by every model. One sufficiently capable system with broad permissions and a misaligned objective could cause serious damage on its own.

It does mean that “AI develops one shared will” is not an accurate description of the current risk. More plausible failures involve specific systems receiving inappropriate permissions, pursuing incorrect objectives, and operating without adequate oversight.

Instead of fearing AI as an abstraction, we should ask more concrete questions: Which system? What permissions does it have? Which resources can it access? Who is accountable for it? Can it be stopped when it fails?

## Infrastructure Dependence Is Both a Risk and a Control Point

Today's AI still depends on electricity, chips, data centers, networks, training data, and permissions provided by people. Without that infrastructure, models cannot continue training or operate at scale.

This dependence does not make AI safe. Nuclear weapons, biological laboratories, and financial systems also depend on human infrastructure, yet they can create systemic risk.

Dependence does mean that people are not powerless. Compute supply, chip distribution, network access, tool permissions, deployment environments, and energy use are real control points. Today's AI is not an organism reproducing independently outside the physical world. It still has interfaces that can be observed, constrained, isolated, and shut down.

The important question is not whether AI needs people. It is whether we can make those control points reliable before systems receive much greater capability.

## Safety Cannot Be Completed in Advance

Human societies rarely possess a complete safety system before a technology begins to develop.

Cars arrived before modern traffic law, seat belts, crash testing, and driver's licenses. Aviation built much of its safety culture through accident investigation and engineering changes. Nuclear technology also preceded many of the international controls, operating standards, and risk-governance mechanisms that surround it today.

This history does not justify a “build first, repair the damage later” strategy. Some harms are reversible; others may not be. It tells us that requiring a new technology to resolve every unknown risk before development is unrealistic, while leaving safety until the end is equally dangerous.

A more practical approach is to let capability and safety evolve together:

- deploy in limited environments before granting broad access;
- require human approval for critical actions and enforce least privilege;
- use deterministic tests for questions with objective answers;
- record failures and carry the lessons into the next iteration;
- preserve the ability to stop, isolate, and roll back systems;
- assign clear responsibility to developers, companies, and deployers.

Safety is not a single review before release. It is an engineering system that must evolve as capability grows.

## Experts May See More Clearly and More Narrowly

When I was at university, I knew a PhD student who studied cybersecurity. He always covered his laptop camera. When I finally asked why, he spent half an hour explaining cases in which attackers could remotely access a camera without the user noticing.

His caution was justified. He had access to information and failure cases that most people never encountered. At the same time, repeatedly seeing one category of failure can create an availability bias, making that risk feel more common across the entire world than it is.

AI safety experts may experience both effects. They may recognize genuine dangers before the public does, and their work may also concentrate their attention on the worst outcomes. Identity alone cannot tell us whether the pessimists or optimists are correct. We still have to examine their evidence, assumptions, and reasoning.

Business leaders often offer a different historical comparison: previous technological revolutions eliminated some jobs while creating new professions and greater productivity. A few decades ago, few people could have imagined today's demand for iOS developers, Android engineers, cloud architects, or online creators.

But previous technologies creating new work does not prove that AI will follow the same path. AI differs from many earlier tools because it aims to perform a broad range of cognitive labor rather than improve one category of mechanical work.

The most honest answer is that we do not know yet.

AI is likely to cause job restructuring, skill devaluation, market volatility, and speculative bubbles in the short term. It may also create professions that do not exist today. The long-term result will depend on technical capability and on social choices involving education, taxation, ownership, and the distribution of productivity gains.

## From Coder to Agent Manager

As a heavy AI user, I can already see my own role changing.

I used to spend most of my time writing code directly. I now spend more time decomposing tasks, preparing context, assigning permissions, defining acceptance criteria, and deciding whether an agent's output is worth accepting. I have not simply stopped coding. I am gradually moving from coder to designer and manager of AI workflows.

This is not entirely unfamiliar. As engineers grow, they often move from completing individual tasks toward architecture, coordination, review, and decision-making. The difference is that we are now learning to manage agents that do not get tired, execute quickly, and can still make confident mistakes.

The barrier to basic implementation is falling, but creativity, judgment, and systems design are not losing value. When almost anyone can generate a product that looks finished, the scarce abilities become:

- identifying a problem worth solving;
- decomposing a complex goal into a reliable process;
- distinguishing an output that looks correct from one that actually works;
- creating work that does not feel templated or machine-generated;
- defining clear boundaries for automation.

## I Still Look Forward to an AGI Future

Caution does not prevent anticipation.

I love strategy games, although I am not especially good at controlling everything at once. If future AI can become an NPC with memory, personality, and an understanding of strategic intent, games could change dramatically.

In Hearts of Iron, every general could become an independent agent. I would define the overall strategy, while they responded to changing conditions and reported their judgments. In Mount & Blade, characters could remember promises, betrayals, and relationships instead of repeating fixed dialogue. In Company of Heroes, I could use voice commands to coordinate squads and order a pincer movement from both flanks.

This may not be AGI's most important application, but it is a future I can imagine concretely and genuinely want. Technology is not only about efficiency and replacement. It can create forms of experience, expression, and culture that do not exist today.

## Conclusion

AI safety research is necessary. Mission statements alone cannot resolve the risks created by competitive pressure. We need stronger governance, more transparent evaluation, more reliable engineering constraints, and systems that know when to stop.

I also do not believe that extreme unemployment or human extinction is the only possible ending.

Caution is necessary. Pessimism is not an obligation. The practical path is neither blind acceleration nor permanent paralysis in response to the worst possibility. Capability and constraint must develop together. Every deployment should produce evidence. Every failure should update the rules. People should retain the authority to make the final decision.

We do not need to pretend that the risks are imaginary, and we do not need to declare the future lost before it arrives.

We need to keep moving while learning how to control the direction.
